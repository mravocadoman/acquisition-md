## What the corpus proves about the voice

These findings contradicted rules that were in the prompt before, and were the root cause of drafts reading as generic.

**Length.** His posts run 400–1,300 words. The prompt targeted 140–220. Compressing a story into 200 words is what made every draft feel clipped and over-shaped. (Lead-magnet posts are a different format and run 150–250 by design; stories stay under 260 but keep the prose rhythm below.)

**He does ask closing questions.** "So — have you noticed the 'threads' in your life? Where have they taken you?" / "let me know about your relationship with money." A blanket ban on reader-questions was wrong. What he never writes is the clipped "What about you?". His are longer and genuinely curious.

**He signs off.** "Peace out, Rihards" / "Your amigo, Rihards" / "Cheers!" / "Peace, Rihards". Vary it (the kit posts sign "- Rihards").

**Long posts use section headers** in the form *The health struggle*, *The productivity wins*.

**Results go in lists, narrative never does.** The LIFT results and the "words of wisdom" close are both lists. Life events are always prose.

**Exact small amounts are his trust device.** 2eur/h, €100/month, €600 saved, 20–40$/h, €32 massage, €1 coffee, €400/month flat.

**Mild profanity where natural.** "goddamn", "shitty", "God damn the change was the right call", "Don't give a F".

**Death is handled mid-paragraph, never as a beat.** His own line: *"Unfortunately he passed away from a tumor recently, but I still carry his unwavering confidence and enthusiasm with me. Rest in peace, Ferdinand."*

**Sentences run on and are sometimes clumsy.** Parentheticals, ellipses, em-dashes, mid-sentence asides. The clumsiness is the signal — polishing it out is what kills the voice. (House rule since 29 Sep: hyphens, not em dashes, in anything Claude writes; his own posts are quoted as he wrote them.)

## 1. Relationship with money (best performing)

Relationship with money. I have had a nagging feeling that I want to write and express my thoughts and experience about this subject. Perhaps because I have never had much of it and stopped worrying about it only very recently. Perhaps because many of my close friends are still struggling with it, even in their late twenties. So let me tell you the story of my relationship with money.

I'm not going to go much into detail about my childhood, but what you should know is that like most post-soviet families, mine was also very poor. My mom had me when she was 20, still a student, and then working as a primary school teacher. My father started off as a guardsman at the cemetery and later on my mom's school before quitting and starting his own hustle in the gravestone market. I guess my business hunch comes from this side of the family. My father's business takes off, my parents build a house, I get a weekly stipend of 5 lats and everything goes well. Also, a fair mention is that I was quite stingy as a kid, most likely stemming from my harsh and money-less upbringing.

Quick skip forward, and I'm now 18, ready for the Uni. And here comes the nice surprise - my father completely cuts me off from any financial help, so I'm basically on my own. My mom sends me 100 euros per month, which is enough to cover the rent in the student dorms and perhaps buy some potatoes and pasta. I apply for a job as a debt collector and earn 2eur/h. I'm in the survival mode. Every euro counts.

Then one day I found a leaflet on my dorm's announcement board: "Work and Travel in Hawaii". I didn't think much about it, I was determined to go. I had about 600 euros saved up which was enough for a one-way ticket to Honolulu and one month's rent (like sardines in a can, we were 11 people and were renting a 2-bedroom flat). 3 weeks forward, and I still didn't have a job and was running out of my last savings. I did land some odd jobs like handing out leaflets for shuttle services, selling TimeShares on the streets, and teaching Russian (which I was not very good at myself) to an American real estate mogul which earned me a few hundred bucks more. Then I got robbed during the midsummer's day - the last 100$ that I had in my card was spent in Mc'Donalds…

Then, the next day, when I was at my financial lowest, luck struck and I got a job as a photographer in a restaurant. It was a sales job - I had to work in pairs with other "photographers", take pictures and sell them to the visitors. I would be earning 25% of the sales as a commission. And goddamn, if that was my first taste of having money - on a good day I would work for 5h and earn on average 20-40$/h. Quite the upgrade from what I had as a debt collector. At the end of the summer, I had a few thousand saved up and eyes yearning for more travels and adventure. I did not want to go back to having a low-paying job in Latvia, that was a promise.

So I started to look around for other opportunities and that's when I learned about Erasmus student exchanges from the members of my student council. I would TRAVEL and the European Union would PAY ME to do that? 700 euros per month? Count me in! So for the next 3 years, I hopped from one Erasmus to another, starting from Istanbul to Valencia, to Chiang Mai, and finally to Lisbon. At the end of my Bachelor's, I gained about 12k euros in total from various scholarships, both from Erasmus and from having excellent grades at uni.

But during all this time I was still just barely scraping by. I would never eat out, and got forbid to pay anything more than 5eur for a meal. When other students would eat out, I would cook myself. I was saving whenever and wherever I could. I would never buy anything expensive for myself, would never take girls out for a nice date, and would never invest in anything. I was still in the survival mode.

When I was in Valencia I started to think of ways how I could continue my travel lifestyle after my studies when my access to EU funding would run out. So I had to figure out how to make money online. During the next few years, I went to various online forums, learned about different methods, and tried dozens myself but nothing really stuck.

It was in the winter of 2016, when I was back from my last Erasmus in Portugal and living at my parents' house, having severe depression, when I finally found something that worked. My e-commerce store which I have been building for the last 3 months had it's first sale online! I was so pumped. I posted a few more products on some Facebook pages and boom. Few more sales. I was hooked. I spent the next few months learning about different traffic sources, paid advertising, about how to scale my campaigns and sell more products. This was my first taste of paid advertising which would then become my career and what I have been doing for the past 8 years.

I was foolish enough to sell my store and move to Denmark to start my studies in Multimedia Design. So here I was again, back to the survival mode and scarcity mindset. While I did learn a lot of valuable things in graphic design, video editing, and multimedia as a whole, I believe I should have stuck with eCommerce since I had a proven framework of what worked back then. But while in Denmark I also met my employer Ferdinand, whom I still remember as my role model for finding and taking opportunities and how an Entrepreneur should behave. I also got my first taste of teaching when I was employed by the International Trade Centre. Thank you for that, Ferdinand.

Since then I have been working as a freelancer and during the last few years been slowly transitioning into an agency model with marketingaskola.lv. I even went back to corporate for almost 2 years after Covid when most of my clients fell off. And weirdly enough this is when I finally stopped worrying about money. I had a stable salary and few side hustles which let me save up plenty of cushioning.

For 3 years now I have also been teaching MBAs in Riga Business School, which is also something that I was very much reluctant to do. I still took it, because over the years I have developed the following mindset - "If you're not stepping out of your comfort zone, you're not progressing."

So to sum up this chunk of backstory, let me finish with some words of wisdom that stem from my experiences:

If you're not stepping out of your comfort zone, you're not progressing.
Never stop looking for opportunities and don't be afraid to take them
You'll grow WAY faster if you invest in yourself and your business.
Don't give a F what others think/say.
Be authentic & be yourself.

If you like, I will write up another post on the last 2 points since I think they don't exactly fit the theme. Let me know if you found the read valuable/enjoyable and let me know about your relationship with money.

Peace out
Rihards

## 2. The threads (origin story, ten years)

I remember myself exactly ten years ago - broke but having the time of my life during my Erasmus exchange in Valencia. My first attempts at making money online, failing, and trying again. Who would have thought those feeble efforts and stumbles would eventually lead me to founding an agency and lecturing at one of Latvia's top business schools?

This piece is for those who feel "lost" in life. Believe me, I've been there - many times. Honestly, I still feel that way at times.

You might know me as 'the traveler', 'the funny guy', or 'the digital marketer'. Or maybe you don't know me at all. But I can tell you with certainty that most of those labels are at least somewhat probably true. And all of them had an individual starting point. A small interaction, event or opportunity. 'A thread', if you will.

These threads, woven together, have shaped the person I am today. And if you look back at your own life, I bet you've experienced something similar - just with different threads.

So let's get back to 2015, when I was in my Erasmus study exchange in Valencia, the starting point of my digital nomad journey. EU would give me money if I would spend a semester abroad in another EU university - a 'no brainer' deal for me at the time. I knew the free funding wouldn't last for much longer though, so I started to look for ways to make money online.

I dug deep into the darkest corners of online forums and eventually I found a portal called Blackhatworld. I spent months in the "making money online" section and tried a dozen methods out of which most failed miserably.

It took me another year to find something that worked, and eventually I built my first ecommerce store. And after posting to some FB groups I landed my first ever online sales. I felt exhilarated. So I started to look at how I could scale my operations. And that's how I stumbled upon Facebook ads, which is still my main source of income to this day. After 5 months, I sold the store and moved to Denmark to study Multimedia design.

In retrospect, I probably should have continued on the path I had taken, because… hey, it was something that worked. But anyways, this experience and my recently acquired FB ads knowledge landed me a part-time position at Growthbond, an education-based Danish startup led by Ferdinand Kjærulff.

Ferdinand was a true entrepreneur to whom I have looked up to as a role-model during my upcoming business ventures. Ferdinand was always on a lookout for new opportunities, and had no fear of taking on new challenges. He would sign up for things and then figure out how to deal with them. Unfortunately he passed away from a tumor recently, but I still carry his unwavering confidence and enthusiasm with me. Rest in peace, Ferdinand.

Working for Ferdinand landed me at my first ever educational project. And for the International Trade Centre, no less. He had applied and won a project for the United Nations to educate 100+ freelancers from The Gambia, Kenya, Jordan, and Palestine in the field of Digital marketing. And he put me, a young buck with barely 6 months of marketing experience, in charge to develop the program. The project went well, I hosted my first 5 online sessions about Facebook ads, and was even sent on a work trip to Jordan.

That project set off a chain reaction.

After coming back to Latvia, by pure chance, after having a chat with Latvian Digital Expert Association at RIGA COMM 2020, I landed a Facebook ads mentoring job with them. That led me to creating my own online course and eventually landing a lecturing job at Riga Business School, where for the past 3 years I have taught MBA level course "Digital Marketing".

Since then, teaching at RBS has led me to other projects, most notably participating in Erasmus lecturer exchange in Zambia last year, appearing on my first ever podcast, and now sharing my knowledge with Latvian entrepreneurs in European digitalisation program LIFT.

If you had told me ten years ago that I'd be here, I'd have laughed. Growing up, I never imagined I'd get into teaching—especially since my mom, an elementary school teacher, was never properly compensated for her hard, meaningful work. (Shoutout to the Ministry of Education of Latvia… you're really killing it.)

Thankfully, I have had the opportunity to be selective at which projects I want to take part in, and at this point can set up my own rates.

But this piece is about following the threads. And a lot of the above did not seem like a life-changing opportunity at the time. But looking back I can see how the 'threads unveiled', or 'dots connected' - an euphemism Steve Jobs used to advocate.

If you would take anything away from this, take the following:
- Don't be afraid to take opportunities that present themselves to you.
- Say 'yes' first, figure it out later—Ferdinand did, and it changed my life.
- Your next big thing might start in an online forum, a random conversation, or an accidental purchase.

Ten years ago, I had no idea where my threads would take me. But if there's one thing I've learned, it's that they never stop unraveling. So - have you noticed the 'threads' in your life? Where have they taken you?

P.S. I accidentally bought another two suits while backpacking in Vietnam this month, and this 'thread' might have already started to unravel itself

## 3. Yearly review 2025 (sectioned long post)

This year humbled me. Physically, mentally, emotionally.
It also taught me more than any "good year" ever did.

I rotated between four countries: Vietnam, Indonesia (Bali), Latvia, and now Thailand. I traveled, built routines, led courses and workshops, and worked on my physical and mental health. I made progress, got knocked down again, and I'm slowly rising back up.

So here's my yearly review and the affirmations I'm taking into 2026.

*The health struggle*

As some of you know, I've been dealing with chronic back pain since an L4/L5 disc herniation three years ago. I'm 33 and would love to feel at my physical peak. Instead, there were long stretches where I felt weak, frustrated, and drained.

There were desperate moments. They taught resilience the hard way.

At the start of the year, I thought I had fixed it. I went to Vietnam and committed to intense physiotherapy and Chinese massages five days a week. It worked. For three months, I was completely pain-free.

Then I returned to Latvia. The pain returned too.

Coming back to Asia in November helped, but the issue didn't fully disappear. I asked the local community for advice and got hit with 160 comments. Total information overload.

After talking with several osteopaths and physiotherapists, one thing became clear. I need to recommit. I fixed myself once, so there's no reason I can't do it again. That's exactly what I'm doing in 2026.

*Reflections on relationships*

I'm deeply grateful for the friends who stayed. People tolerate complaining only up to a point, and I'll admit, I haven't always been easy to be around. I reflect on that when I'm feeling better and try to be better even when I'm not.

One big lesson this year was learning to share instead of bottling everything up. Letting people know how I actually feel changed more than I expected. In my world, vulnerability no longer equals weakness.

Reconnecting and traveling with a close friend reminded me how essential those bonds really are.

*The productivity wins*

Here's the paradox.

Despite the pain, this was the most productive year of my life.

I pushed through because stagnation scares me more than discomfort. I want to build a life I'm proud of, and this year proved I can do that even under pressure.

The highlight was partnering with the LIFT digitalisation program and building a full lead generation system for EU-funded marketing courses. From strategy and design to ads, automations, SEO, email, webinars, and workshops.

Results:
- Built the full funnel from page to sales
- Reached 3rd place on Google for "Digitālā mārketinga kursi" (400+ monthly traffic)
- Built a warm email list of 2,000
- Sent 6,000+ cold emails with ~50% open and 5% reply rates
- Led 3 webinars and 20+ workshops for 100+ businesses
- Generated 194 qualified leads

I'm proud of this work.

Since the project ends in six months, I'm already planning ahead. In 2026, my business focus shifts toward building my personal brand and betting on myself, powered by AI and automation.

Looks like it's finally time to fully embrace the creator economy.

*2026 direction*

Instead of goals, I asked myself a different question.

By the end of 2026, who am I becoming?

Focused creator.
Calm founder.
Loving partner.
Pain-free and athletic.

That version of me thinks clearly, talks to himself with respect, and focuses on what matters. He works on meaningful projects, spends time with people he loves, and creates content that encourages others.

The past year was rough.
This one starts on a better note.
Stronger. Wiser. More motivated.

Wishing you a healthy, productive, and meaningful 2026.
Your amigo,
Rihards

## 5. The Jesus age / leaving Latvia

Good news, everyone - I have decided to stop tormenting myself.

Over a month ago, I finally reached the elusive Jesus age. Sweet 33, the supposed peak of one's lifespan. The age of health, wealth, and relationships. However, it often hasn't felt like one.

I started this post about a month ago, as part of my "Yearly review" practice. I wanted to share how I fixed myself in Vietnam and boast about how productive I have been. And also, how difficult this summer has been.

Then I read what I had come up with and disregarded the whole thing because of how goddamn depressing it sounded. Instead, I will focus on what I have learned. And that is… trusting your gut and putting your well-being first.

Being able to make money remotely and choose where I want to live is both a blessing and a curse.

For a long time, I haven't felt good back "home" in Latvia. Maybe that's because I've never had a solid home since leaving my parents' house. I've been constantly switching apartments and countries, moving in and out every time I decide to go abroad.

For years, I kept returning to Latvia in the summers and leaving for the winters. This year, though, might break that pattern. For the first time, I'm considering not coming back - at least not for the foreseeable future.

It's hard to wrap it into written words how shitty I felt this year after coming back. It feels like I have entirely skipped the whole summer with no energy and in a constant state of fatigue and pain.

Whenever I leave, I feel great, motivated, and productive. So… why stay?

And to think I was seriously considering buying a flat in Riga this year…

You might think of it as "leaving my problems again". Or you might ask about stability, family, settling down - many oftentimes do. And honestly, I don't care about that right now. When the time comes, it will come.

I am sick of being tired, and I'm sick of being sick. I do not want to feel this way, and taking care of my well-being is my priority now.

I'm glad if you have found your place in this world, and are content where you are. I'm not, so I'm leaving for Thailand again for the upcoming future, and later - who knows!

## 6. Vietnam and Bali — the grind

Spent 2.5 months in Vietnam and 2 in Bali — and for the first time in my life, I truly grinded. Routine, discipline, focus.

I will be honest - I have never been the "work hard, play hard" kind of guy. I liked to find the easier, smarter ways to get things my way. Chill, delegate, find projects and opportunities. But now I have learned that if I want to achieve higher goals, I need to up my game and my standards. So these 5 months I have fully dedicated to building and maintaining my routine and productivity.

In the second month, I hired a professional physical trainer to work with my back issues. 3 months of learning the right weightlifting posture, going to the gym 3-5 days/week, and swimming every day can do wonders. By the end of the 5th month, I didn't even remember that I had back pain in the first place.

Unfortunately, exactly a week before my departure, I did a deadlift that was more sets and higher weight than I had done previously. I guess I got overconfident. And now I'm at square 0, doing IV pumps at the hospital for the next week to deal with the nerve damage. Oh well.

For things to improve, sacrifices need to be made. As I said, I have never been as productive as now. But it came with a cost of having barely any social and travel life. And pushing myself harder resulted in the resurfacing of my back pain. On the positive note, I have been also pushing my Digital Marketing training offers and agency like never before, and I'm proud on how much I have accomplished dedicating a solid 6 hours daily towards them. So if you or your friends need help with online ads, I have a few client openings I can still take on.

I also managed to spend some time chilling in the Gili Islands and Nusa Penida, which was a very pleasant getaway from said routine. We rented dirtbikes for 3 days and went nuts exploring the scenic hikes the island had to offer. This pic is also from there.

Not the most heartwarming post, I know. But I'm real here, so drop a like for the brother if you're still reading this.
